# Analysing the Factors Affecting Performance in Word Sense Disambiguation Systems

This project investigates why Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD) systems have plateaued at 80–85% accuracy despite continued advances in transformer-based architectures. It evaluates two representative systems, GlossBERT and EWISER, on the complete SemCor corpus (229,532 instances) - around 30 times larger than the SemEval benchmarks typically used to report these systems' accuracy - to examine whether the plateau comes from the models themselves, from the quality of SemCor's annotations, or from the inherent granularity of WordNet's sense distinctions.

A flowchart-based error classification method was built systematically from Open English WordNet 2025 sense definitions and used to sort model errors into three causes: genuine model failure, SemCor annotation error, and cases where two WordNet senses are simultaneously valid in context. Alongside this, a systematic encoding mismatch was discovered between the WordNet format SemCor was originally annotated in and the format used by both models during inference, which had been silently inflating both systems' measured error rates. Correcting it raises GlossBERT's accuracy from 84.25% to 90.35% and EWISER's from 79.14% to 82.82%.

The analysis was extended from light verbs to the adjective "good" to test whether the granularity problem is worse for some word classes than others, and the results were cross-checked against an independent, large-scale study that relabelled SemCor using large language models. Together, the findings suggest that the long-standing WSD accuracy ceiling is not purely architectural - it reflects a combination of correctable data artefacts, genuine inconsistencies in how SemCor was annotated, and a real, irreducible granularity problem in how finely WordNet distinguishes certain senses.

The main dataset is SemCor, a corpus of Brown Corpus text manually annotated with WordNet senses by Miller et al. in 1993, accessed here through the UFSAC framework, which unifies sense-annotated corpora into a common format. Sense definitions and the sense inventory used for both model inference and the flowchart classification come from Open English WordNet 2025. A second, external dataset - a large-scale relabelling of the complete SemCor corpus using GPT-5.5 and Gemma 4 31B, produced by a separate research group - is used as independent corroborating evidence rather than as training or evaluation data of its own.

The project builds directly on prior published work in this area, including Miller et al.'s original SemCor paper, Navigli's widely cited survey of word sense disambiguation, Miller's foundational WordNet paper, and the two model papers this study evaluates: GlossBERT by Huang et al. and EWISER by Bevilacqua and Navigli. It also draws on Raganato et al.'s standardised WSD evaluation framework, Kilgarriff's early argument that many WordNet sense distinctions are unreliable, and several more recent papers on sense granularity, corpus quality, and knowledge-graph-based disambiguation methods, all of which are cited in full in the accompanying paper.
